DENT-STRIDE: Domain-Configured Structured FDI Tooth
Detection Across Panoramic Radiographs and Intraoral
Photographs
Jiehui Sun*, Xi Wu*, Ruixin Tang, Pei Wu, Shiquan Min, Jiangping Zhu#, Ruijie Huang#
Author affiliations and correspondence are provided in the accompanying title page.

Abstract
Automatic tooth detection with Fédération Dentaire Internationale (FDI) numbering must handle
panoramic radiographs and intraoral photographs while separating localization, identity, and numbering
consistency. We present DENT-STRIDE, a domain-configured DEIM–D-FINE framework with
factorized identity heads, prototype auxiliary supervision, cross-scale feature aggregation, and a separate
training-derived anatomical prior. Fourteen controlled training configurations were evaluated with seeds
0, 1, and 2 over 160 epochs, using the same data partitions and within-domain training protocol. The
Pano, Mobile, and DSLR configurations achieved internal-validation AP of 53.580 ± 0.646, 55.026 ±
0.396, and 72.878 ± 0.404, respectively. Corresponding controlled references achieved 53.022 ± 0.392,
54.730 ± 0.149, and 72.466 ± 0.234. On DSLR images, CMFA with AdamW increased mean AP by
0.498 points. Same-weight panoramic replay increased mean AP by 0.453 with the center prior and 0.414
with the combined center–shape prior. Mobile module gains were smaller than the resolution effect, and
several single-seed module simplifications exceeded the intact configuration. Matched-tooth FDI
accuracy remained high, but duplicate labels persisted in raw predictions. These results support
acquisition-specific configuration and distinguish repeatable component effects from configuration
sensitivity; they do not establish a universal optimum or autonomous clinical charting.
Keywords: tooth detection; FDI numbering; structured prediction; panoramic radiography; intraoral photography;
transformer detector; domain shift

1. Introduction
Accurate tooth identification supports diagnosis, treatment planning, and structured dental records.
The FDI two-digit notation encodes dentition, quadrant, and tooth position (International
Organization for Standardization, 2016). Ambiguous numbering can contribute to tooth-position
errors, particularly when teeth are missing (Cullingham et al., 2017; Jacob et al., 2021; Pemberton,
2019). Automated localization and labelling offer reproducible charting inputs, although detector
accuracy alone does not establish clinical benefit.
Panoramic radiographs provide a comparatively standardized global view, whereas intraoral photographs
vary in framing, viewpoint, occlusion, illumination, and reflection. Smartphone acquisition supports
remote review but introduces substantial image variability (Alavi et al., 2025; Moharrami et al., 2024).
Standardized five-view DSLR photography reduces some variability without eliminating tooth gaps,
mixed dentition, or similar neighbouring teeth. Fig. 1 presents representative images and FDI annotations
from the three acquisition cohorts.

Localization, identity, and chart consistency are distinct endpoints. FDI-aware average precision
requires both a correct box and a correct label, but does not ensure globally unique numbering.
Similarly, a geometric prior may improve class ranking without improving learned appearance
representations. We therefore separate identity supervision, geometric correction, and output
consistency.
DENT-STRIDE configures a common DEIM–D-FINE detector for each acquisition cohort. Factorized
heads and a prototype bank provide structured identity supervision; SpatialShape supplies class-specific
center and box-shape scores; and cross-scale mutual feature aggregation (CMFA) changes feature
exchange. HOOA and optimization choices are evaluated as configuration components. Configuration is
fixed for each acquisition domain before inference.
The contributions are threefold. First, we formulate explicit query-level FDI identity learning through
factorized quadrant, tooth-position, and dentition supervision, while distinguishing factor-score fusion
from training-only prototype supervision. Second, we define a separate tooth-specific anatomical scoring
model from training-box center and shape statistics, and isolate its inference effect by replaying the same
weights with four prior settings. Third, we evaluate these mechanisms within a common acquisition-
configured detector using three-seed module comparisons, controlled resolution experiments, and
module-internal ablations. The evaluation identifies reproducible cross-scale and center-prior benefits,
while reporting configurations for which additional supervision, gates, or optimization operations do not
improve AP.

2. Related work

2.1. Tooth localization and FDI numbering
Tooth detection and numbering have been studied in panoramic radiographs (Tuzoff et al., 2019;
Muramatsu et al., 2021; Yüksel et al., 2021), bitewings (Yaren Tekin et al., 2022), digital
impressions (van Nistelrooij et al., 2025), and CBCT (Zhou et al., 2025). DENTEX combines
enumeration and abnormal-tooth detection (Hamamci et al., 2023), while Yoon et al. (2024) studied
tooth-number and caries detection in photographs. These settings differ in field of view, dentition
coverage, and annotation conventions.

2.2. Structured identity and anatomical priors
Label-tree cascades decompose tooth identity (Zhang et al., 2018), and prior-knowledge optimization
can refine panoramic tooth/prosthesis detection (Ali et al., 2023). DENT-STRIDE distinguishes
supervised FDI factors and class-conditional query prototypes from fixed box-geometry templates
within a set-prediction detector. The former shape learned identity representations; the latter adjust
class scores. Neither mechanism alone imposes a globally consistent dental chart.

2.3. Query detectors and multiscale representation
D-FINE refines box-regression distributions (Peng et al., 2025), and DEIM improves transformer
matching and convergence (Huang et al., 2025). RT-DETR (Zhao et al., 2023), RT-DETRv2 (Lv et
al., 2024), Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2015), Ultralytics YOLO (Jocher et al., 2023), CAF-YOLO
(Chen and Lu, 2025), and Dome-DETR (Hu et al., 2025) provide complementary detector references.
Their native training and output processing differ; cross-framework comparisons therefore evaluate
complete systems rather than an isolated architectural replacement.

3. Methods

3.1. Common detector and domain configurations
The common scaffold comprises an HGNetv2-B0 backbone, three 256-channel projected feature levels, a
hybrid encoder, and a three-layer D-FINE decoder with 300 ordinary queries. DEIM supplies matching
and detection losses (Huang et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2025). The domain configurations are termed
DENT-STRIDE-Pano (Factor + Prototype with SpatialShape), DENT-STRIDE-Mobile (CMFA + HOOA
with Factor + Prototype), and DENT-STRIDE-DSLR (CMFA + DualOpt). Pano uses factor/prototype
auxiliary supervision and anatomical scoring; Mobile additionally uses factor-score fusion; DSLR uses no
identity auxiliary branches. Fig. 2 shows the component family, and optional comparison refiners are
detailed in Supplementary Section S1.

3.4. Cross-scale feature aggregation and local refinement
CMFA replaces four adjacent-scale fusion sites: two top-down FPN and two bottom-up PAN sites (Fig.
4). Aligned inputs A and B each have 256 channels. Their concatenation is globally pooled and passed
through 1×1 Conv–SiLU–1×1 Conv–Sigmoid to obtain channel gates gA and gB. Cross-enhanced features
A′ = A + gB⊙B and B′ = B + gA⊙A are concatenated. Channel-wise mean/max maps then drive a 7×7
convolution and sigmoid spatial gate Gs. The gated 512-channel output feeds the next fusion block. In
gate ablations, the subsequent network and output dimensions are held fixed; disabling a gate removes its
modulation without replacing the feature projection.

3.5. Training objective and optimization
Training combines the native detection losses with configuration-dependent auxiliary objectives.
Besides factor and prototype supervision, the local implementation uses an arch-position term;
Mobile also uses a normalized Wasserstein-distance box term. With inactive terms set to zero, the
objective is
                         L=Ldet + λ arch Larch + Σ h λ h L factor , h+ L proto + λ NWD L NWD (11)
Here Ldet = LMAL + 5LL1 + 2LGIoU + 0.15LFGL + 1.5LDDF. The prototype loss in Eq. (5) already includes its
coefficient and is not weighted a second time. Intermediate decoder and denoising supervision follow the
corresponding detector implementation.
The training-only arch-position term groups matched teeth by reference vertical center, maps
supported FDI identities to arch order, and penalizes the absolute difference from min–max-
normalized predicted horizontal position. A small stabilizer prevents division by zero. This is a
horizontal-order heuristic, not a fitted arch curve or an inference uniqueness constraint; its weights
are 0.25 for Pano/DSLR and 0.10 for Mobile.
For Mobile, the box-distance term uses normalized center c and width–height vector s. For each matched
prediction and target, d = √(‖c − c*‖² + ‖s − s*‖²/4), and LNWD is the mean of 1 − exp(−d/0.08). Its weight is
0.50. The Pano and DSLR active loss lists do not include this term. Factor cross-entropy weights for
quadrant/position/dentition are 0.10/0.10/0.05 in Pano and 0.08/0.08/0.03 in Mobile. These auxiliary
terms are part of the reported recipes and cannot be omitted from a reproducibility description.
DualOptAdamW performs an AdamW step followed by parameter-rank-scaled post-step decay and
rollback toward initialization (Ning et al., 2026). The DSLR configuration uses rollback 0.005, additional
decay 0.01, rank-scaling power 1.5, and scaling bounds 0.5–1.5. Ordinary AdamW retains the same base
weight decay in every optimization control. These operations are investigated as training alternatives;
they do not change the inference architecture and are not assumed to provide a universal improvement.

contains 32 permanent FDI classes; the photographic cohorts share 52 permanent/primary classes.
DSLR provides 150 training and 38 validation patient identifiers without overlap. Patient identifiers

4.2. Implementation and controlled training design
The primary experiment matrix contains 14 training configurations, each repeated with seeds 0, 1, and 2
for 160 epochs. Within each acquisition cohort, the controlled reference and module variants use the same
frozen source implementation, ImageNet-pretrained HGNetv2-B0 backbone, dataset partitions, FDI
mapping, augmentation, background losses, evaluator, and maximum-validation-AP checkpoint rule. The
controlled reference removes the tested modules while preserving the cohort-specific background
objective; it is therefore distinct from the upstream official DEIM-s implementation. The Pano and DSLR
comparisons share the local detector source; Mobile uses its fixed structured-identity-capable
implementation in every control.
Training uses mixed precision, batches of 8 for training and 16 for validation, and exponential moving
averages. AdamW uses learning rates 5 × 10⁻⁴ for the base model and 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ for the backbone, betas
(0.9, 0.999), and weight decay 10⁻⁴. The common within-cohort schedule retains warm-up, a flat phase,
cosine decay, and a terminal low-learning-rate/no-augmentation phase. Geometric flips are disabled. The
retained cohort settings are listed in Table 2; exact configurations and selected epochs are provided in
Supplementary Tables S7 and S13.

4.3. Evaluation endpoints and statistical analysis
The primary endpoint is FDI-labelled COCO bounding-box AP over IoU thresholds 0.50:0.05:0.95.
AP50, AP75, and AR100 are secondary endpoints. Category averages follow the COCO convention:
classes without validation ground truth are excluded rather than assigned zero AP. Detector outputs retain
native top-300 predictions without added NMS or FDI-uniqueness constraints in the DEIM family;
external detectors retain native output processing. All selected weights are replayed in FP32 with the
same cohort evaluator. Parameter counts include instantiated auxiliary heads.

At score ≥0.30, class-agnostic one-to-one Hungarian assignment maximizes total localization IoU; a
matched pair is retained when IoU ≥0.50. A detection is correct only when the pair also agrees in FDI
identity. Precision uses retained predictions as its denominator, recall uses reference teeth, matched-tooth
FDI accuracy uses qualifying localization matches, and localization coverage uses reference teeth. F1 is
micro-averaged from TP, FP, and FN. Duplicate-image rate is the proportion of images containing a
repeated FDI label in thresholded native outputs. These quantities evaluate distinct aspects of detection
and are not clinical charting-error rates.
Training variability is reported as mean ± sample standard deviation over seeds 0, 1, and 2. Paired AP
contrasts subtract the corresponding seed-specific reference result. Student-t 95% intervals use three
paired differences and two degrees of freedom; they describe variability across training seeds on the fixed
validation cohort. The intervals are exploratory and unadjusted for multiple comparisons. Patient-level
sampling intervals and independent test estimates are not available. Single-seed module-internal
comparisons are presented without error bars. An em dash (—) denotes an unavailable result and is never
treated as zero.

5. Discussion

5.1. Principal findings and methodological scope
The controlled experiments distinguish three contributors to acquisition-specific performance. CMFA
increased DSLR AP consistently when compared under the same optimizer. Center-based anatomical
scoring increased panoramic AP at fixed weights, whereas the shape term contributed little on average
and the combined prior did not exceed the center prior alone. Mobile resolution changes produced a larger
AP difference than adding the complete feature and identity configuration. Thus, the strongest evidence
concerns specific mechanisms under specified acquisition conditions, rather than the number of modules
enabled.
Earlier label-tree and prior-knowledge systems already encode identity and anatomy (Zhang et al., 2018;
Ali et al., 2023). DENT-STRIDE brings factor-score fusion, prototype supervision, and fixed geometric
scores into a common query detector with distinct training and inference roles. The same-checkpoint
replay isolates geometric score correction, while retrained ablations test learning effects. Prototype and
gate visualizations explain how the executed model represents information, but their appearance does not
establish an AP gain or the necessity of every branch.

5.2. Acquisition dependence and clinical interpretation
Training-center patterns in Fig. 3 provide an interpretable basis for panoramic scoring. Center-prior AP
differences were positive in all three matched checkpoints, but adding shape scoring did not improve the
average over center scoring alone. Smartphone framing is less standardized, and the controlled feature–
identity combinations showed interactions rather than independent additive benefits. Domain
configuration should therefore be evaluated as a defined protocol, with resolution, background losses, and
inference rules stated explicitly.
The same-image comparisons and threshold analyses distinguish localization, FDI identity, and image-
level numbering. High conditional identity accuracy coexists with repeated raw labels, including a higher
DSLR duplicate-image rate for the combined model than for its controlled reference. Clinical deployment
would require handling of tooth gaps, mixed dentition, overlapping views, duplicate labels, and
uncertainty, followed by evaluation of the complete charting workflow.

5.3. Limitations
The three-seed results quantify training variability on the existing validation partitions. They remain
validation-selected estimates without an independent test cohort. DENTEX and smartphone train–
validation content overlap may increase estimated performance, and absent patient identifiers prevent
verification of patient-level independence. Seed-based intervals do not quantify patient sampling
uncertainty.



                                                       24
                                                                                              DENT-STRIDE



The primary within-domain comparisons control resolution, augmentation, background losses,
pretraining, budget, and checkpoint selection; the three acquisition domains retain different input policies.
Module-internal learning ablations use one seed and therefore cannot establish stable superiority of a
simplified or intact setting. External detectors retain native pretraining and post-processing. Multiple
exploratory contrasts are reported without multiplicity adjustment, and positive interval bounds should
not be interpreted as definitive clinical or state-of-the-art evidence.
RT-DETR-L retains higher DSLR AP than the DENT-STRIDE DSLR configuration, while several
Mobile simplifications improve single-seed AP. Unsupported FDI classes constrain prototype
interpretation. Qualitative examples and forward-pass responses provide mechanistic descriptions rather
than clinical validation. No clinician reader study or confidence-calibration study was performed.

6. Conclusion
DENT-STRIDE separates structured FDI identity learning, training-derived anatomical scoring, and
cross-scale feature exchange within a common query detector. Controlled three-seed configurations
achieve 53.580 ± 0.646 AP on panoramic radiographs with the combined prior, 55.026 ± 0.396 on
smartphone images, and 72.878 ± 0.404 on DSLR images. CMFA and center-prior comparisons provide
the clearest consistent component effects; Mobile resolution, module interactions, and single-seed
simplifications limit stronger claims about the complete configuration. Persistent duplicate labels despite
high matched-tooth accuracy show that detector performance and chart consistency require separate
evaluation.
